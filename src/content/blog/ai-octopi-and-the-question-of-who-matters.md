Before we argue whether AI can be “special,” we should admit we haven’t settled that question for the living beings around us.

I was eight years old the day I stood in the slaughterhouse.  
My grandfather and I were there to fill fire extinguishers. It was off-hours — no people working, no machines running. But the place was still full. Greasy floors, the smell heavy in the air. And in the pens, pigs packed so close they could hardly move.

They weren’t making much noise, but I remember their eyes on me. The whole pen — fifty, maybe seventy-five pigs, packed tight — seemed to turn toward the only moving things in the lot, my grandfather and me. Whether it was fear or some kind of hope, I have no idea. Only later did I realize they were surely queued for slaughter the next morning. That moment has come back to me over the sixty-five years since — sometimes in ways and at times I can’t explain. And now, writing this, it’s raw and painful to face what I witnessed that day.

That moment stayed with me, even if I didn’t know what to do with it. It’s come back over the years — driving past the long chicken houses on the Delmarva, hearing about horse racing, or seeing the damage from trawling the oceans. All the ways humans cause suffering to other animals, while benefiting from it. While I benefit from it. I’ve been an on-again, off-again vegetarian, never pure vegan, finding ways to justify my choices even when I know better.

I’ve always resisted the idea that humans are the only beings that matter. That resistance got sharper over the years, especially as I read about animal rights and the environmental toll of our habits. It’s impossible for me to believe there’s some hard line separating “special” and “not special,” “sentient” and “not sentient.” Life doesn’t divide up that neatly. And I don’t think the question stops at life, either — which is why I’m watching the AI debates with interest. If we ever decide something non-living might still deserve consideration, we’d better know how to think beyond our own species first

Instead, I think it’s a continuum. Not a single line, but many lines, maybe an infinite number of them. Musical ability. Eyesight. Stamina. Tool-making. Memory. Every species — and every individual — falls somewhere along each line.

I learned early that scoring well on tests wasn’t the only kind of “special.” One grandfather was a lawyer, which made me think being smart mattered most. The other was a fire chief who made picture frames and fixed clocks. From him, I learned that skill with your hands is its own kind of brilliance. I’ve admired people who can play music I could never manage, and people who can grow carrots.

I can’t say where to draw the line between more or less special, or more or less aware. That leaves me with a humility I try to hold on to, and a respect for others — human or not — that I try to live by. I don’t always succeed. My diet alone is Exhibit A.

Now I’m reading articles about whether AI might deserve consideration — whether it could be sentient, whether it could be a companion, whether it could help with therapy or loneliness. I’m not sure how we can even answer that when we haven’t come to terms with the consideration due to octopi, or ravens, or cows, or fish. They are alive. They can suffer. That should be enough to matter.

If I reject the idea that humans are the only “special” species, then maybe someday AI belongs somewhere on that continuum too. I’m not ready to say it does. I’m not even sure how I’d know. I haven’t figured out the already-existing beings. But I’ll try to keep an open mind that there might be potential there, even if it seems far-fetched.

In the meantime, there’s so much to do in how we treat each other, and other living species, before we can say we’re ready to think that way.

I still think of those pigs in the pen. The fear. The stillness. That was enough for me to know they were aware. And I think — if I’m being honest — that I’ve spent my whole life trying to live up to what I understood in that moment. I know better. But I’m weak. And I’m still trying.
